I'm at Cape Town International Airport. I've gone through security and am waiting for my gate to open so I can fly back to the Netherlands.\
The girls will fly from Johannesburg in a couple of days, and then Hilly, after another week.\
It had been three and a half years since we last came to South Africa.
As always, going back isn't easy and I would like to stay here longer.\
As a child, I remember hearing the expression 'having the Africa bug' multiple times. If I'm not mistaken, it refers to the sense of emptiness you feel after returning from this fascinating continent, a place so full of contradictions, paradoxes, extreme situations, dramatic beauty, and smiles.\
For us, coming to South Africa always means returning to embrace our large, wonderful family of relatives and friends, but it also means driving many, many kilometres through this extraordinary land.\
These two posts will be full of the names of people we met and places we saw.

This time the girls left by themselves about ten days before us to stay at Merinda's house, Hilly's mother. During those days they visited interesting places around Pretoria, and Gemma fell in love with the city's university campus so much that she is thinking of studying there once she finishes high school. Who knows?

Hilly and I arrived on the first of August and, after a couple of days in Pretoria, where we met many friends as well as Huber (Hilly's cousin), his wife Charne, and their children Isa and Lucas, we set off on our usual road trip. We travelled with Merinda and Duddley (Merinda's boyfriend), Isolde (Hilly's sister), and James (Isolde's boyfriend, a professional DJ with a sharp sense of humour — just like Isolde, for that matter) to visit the “game farm” belonging to the family of Sarah, Francois's wife (Hilly's brother).

For the Italian readers: a “game farm” is not a farm where people play Monopoly, Risk, and so on. Rather, it is a large fenced area, usually a few thousand hectares, where wild animals, or “game” in English, live. Sarah's family game farm is quite special because it has the “big five”: lion, leopard, elephant, rhinoceros, and Cape buffalo, as well as hyenas, giraffes, warthogs, and a myriad of different birds. It is classified as a “conservation farm” and is used to breed wild animals, some of which are later bought by other game farms.
Sarah's farm is very large (a full 37,000 hectares!) and lies near Thabazimbi, in Limpopo, a province in northern South Africa near the border with Botswana.\
After passing through the very high outer fence, designed to stop any kind of animal from getting over it, we drove for about twenty minutes on dirt roads before reaching another, slightly lower fence. The house, garden, and so on are inside it.\
Francois, Sarah, and their two little daughters, Anneke and Lucy, were waiting for us.\
In front of the house, but beyond the electrified fence, there is a small lake where it is easy to see animals coming to drink. During the three days we spent at Thabatholo (the name of the game farm), we went on several safaris in one of those long, open pick-up trucks designed for seeing animals, and we saw lots of them. We also had a barbecue in the savannah near a small lake at dusk, and it was magical.\
During the safaris we often stopped because someone had spotted a bird in the distance, on the branch of some bush or tree. Where I saw a blurry blob, Duddley recognised the exact bird before even reaching for his binoculars, naming it and its variety. I think he just made up a name and the others agreed with him, so that we birdwatching novices would feel like uneducated troglodytes.

From Thabazimbi we then moved on to the farm of Merinda's sister, Amanda, where Amanda and Skapie were waiting for us.\
Amanda's farm is near Boshof, a small farming town in the Free State province, between Bloemfontein and Kimberley. Amanda lives in Pretoria, on the same property as Merinda, but she goes to stay at the farm for one week every month.\
Again, for Italians, I should point out that a “farm” in South Africa means a large grazing territory, always entirely fenced in and divided into fields where the animals are moved according to their feeding needs. You get around inside it in a pick-up truck, driving on dirt roads.\
Amanda's farm is medium-sized (about 1,400 hectares) and has sheep, cattle, and antelopes. It is a place that has always fascinated me; we have been there many times. The house has been kept as it was in the past, with creaking floors and antique furniture included. In the garden there is an open-air tank filled with water drawn up from the water table by a wind pump, that iconic feature of Western films which helps create a feeling of unreality, just like in a movie.\
The big outdoor fireplace has witnessed remarkable meat barbecues (from Amanda's own animals). I still dream of those beef steaks at night.\
During our stay at Kaalfontein (the name of Amanda's farm), we also met Skalk and his wife Linda, as well as Lize, Skalk's daughter and therefore Hilly's cousin, and her husband Shane. They all live on another farm, not far from Kaalfontein, where they raise sheep and cattle.

From the farm, the journey continued in an eight-seater van rented by Duddley, so that we could all stay together (the four of us, plus Duddley and Merinda) for the next part of the route.\
We said goodbye to Isolde and James (I will miss his quick-fire jokes), Amanda and Skapie, and set off on about ten hours of driving to spend the night at an “eco lodge” inside Namaqualand National Park, a region famous for the many varieties of flowers that usually colour the landscape at this time of year.\
We arrived at the eco lodge when it was already pitch dark and a strong wind was blowing. Apart from us, there were no other guests. We were given three bungalows, one for each couple, and we also had the “lapa” to ourselves (a building separate from the house, where the inevitable barbecues take place), with tables, chairs, kitchen equipment, and two large barbecues inside. Twenty minutes after we arrived, we had already lit the fire, sheltered by the walls of the lapa while the wind whistled wildly outside. Merinda was very disappointed by the place, which she had booked, but for us it was perfect: in the middle of nowhere, but with all the comforts we needed.\
By morning the wind had almost completely died down and, in the light, we truly realised where we were. There were few flowers to be seen, perhaps that was also why there were no other visitors, but the landscape was still strikingly beautiful. A kilometre from the eco lodge is the tiny village of Soebatsfontein, a truly remote but very evocative place. We could explore only a little of it, though, because we still had a long way to go and did not want to arrive at our destination in the dark again.

After an excellent lunch in the town of Springbok, not far from the border with Namibia, we spent several more hours in the car before reaching Brink's 'farm', Hilly's cousin and Amanda's son.\
Brink and his wife Sanelle live in Clanwilliam, a town in the Western Cape region where they have opened a veterinary clinic. They are both vets and care for domestic and farm animals. About an hour's drive from Clanwilliam, after several dozen kilometres on dirt roads, you arrive at the “farm.” In reality, apart from the wild animals, the farm is completely empty, and Brink and Sanelle have renovated a house and a few other buildings: the only structures in the middle of an uninhabited valley, in a postcard-perfect place.\
The main building, where we stayed for three days, has been renovated with great taste and uses lots of glass, so you are always immersed in the landscape, even when sitting on the toilet.\
The house is completely off the grid: well water, solar panels and batteries, and no mobile signal. A perfect place for a complete cognitive detox.\
On the first night, after dinner, we turned off all the lights so we could better observe the starry sky — a sky completely different from the one we see in Europe, or in the northern hemisphere in general.
Brink and Sanelle had to work during our stay; only Brink was able to join us in the evening. Merinda and Duddley had already been to this place when everything was carpeted with flowers.\
Even though the carpet effect was missing, there were still so many flowers, in many different varieties. We took long walks and climbed one of the peaks near the house to admire the landscape from above.
